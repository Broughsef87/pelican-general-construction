Why post-frame costs less

It isn't a cheaper building. It's a different way of carrying the load, and the savings come from what you don't have to do.

  • No continuous foundation wall. Large wood columns carry the roof and wall loads directly, so there's no perimeter footing and stem wall to form and pour.
  • Concrete is optional. Pour a full slab, do partial concrete where you need it, or run compacted gravel. Your call, and it's the biggest lever on the budget.
  • Fewer framing members, longer spans. Columns spaced further apart mean less material and less labor for the same footprint.
  • Faster to dry-in. Fewer steps between breaking ground and a weathertight building.
  • Easy to insulate and finish. The deep wall cavity between columns takes insulation without furring anything out.

Pole building or steel building?

This is the question we get most, and the honest answer depends on span and use — not on which one is "better."

Post-frame usually wins for shops, storage, agricultural buildings and garages at moderate spans. It's less expensive to build, quicker to put up, and simpler to insulate. If the building's job is to cover equipment, house a shop, or store product, post-frame is normally the efficient choice.

Pre-engineered steel usually wins when you need wide clear spans with no interior columns, heavier roof or crane loads, or a finished commercial appearance for a customer-facing building. If you're weighing that side, our steel and metal buildings page covers it in detail.

Not sure which describes your project? Tell us what the building needs to do and we'll tell you which way we'd go — including when that means the less expensive option.

How we build it

  1. Site walk and scope. We look at access, drainage, setbacks and what you actually need the building to do before anyone quotes a number.
  2. Permitting and engineering. Post-frame still has to meet Washington code for wind and snow loads. We handle the drawings and the permit.
  3. Site prep and columns. Grading, then columns set to depth and braced plumb — the step everything else depends on.
  4. Frame and dry-in. Trusses, purlins, roofing and siding. This is the part that goes up fast.
  5. Concrete, doors and finish. Slab if you're pouring one, overhead and entry doors, insulation, interior finish, wiring rough-in coordination.
  6. Walk-through and close-out. One crew, one point of contact, straight answers the whole way through.

FAQ

What exactly is a pole building?
A pole building — also called post-frame — carries its load on large wood columns rather than on a continuous foundation wall. That single difference is why it's faster and less expensive to build than conventional or steel construction for the same square footage.
Do I need a concrete slab?
Not necessarily. Because the columns carry the load, you can pour a full slab, do partial concrete where you need it, or leave compacted gravel. It's usually the biggest single decision in the budget, and we'll walk you through the trade-offs.
Can it be insulated and heated?
Yes, and it's common. The deep wall cavity between columns takes insulation easily. Heated, insulated shops are one of the most frequent post-frame projects we build.
How long does a pole building take?
The structure itself goes up quickly — that's the main appeal. Realistically the schedule is driven by permitting, site prep and concrete, not by framing. We'll give you a real timeline after a site review rather than a number over the phone.
Do you handle permits?
Yes. Engineering, drawings and permitting are part of what we do. Post-frame still has to meet Washington wind and snow load requirements.
What does a pole building cost?
It depends heavily on span, height, concrete and finish level — which is why we won't quote a per-square-foot number that turns out to be wrong. Our Tri-Cities cost guide explains what actually drives the number.
